**Murphy:** *English Grammar in Use*, unit 121 (at / on / in: time), section C: no *at / on / in* before *last / next / this / every* (*last Monday, this weekend, every morning*); the same unit's note on *tomorrow / yesterday* without a preposition.

**What the point had:** 3 choices (*___ tomorrow*, *___ last Friday*, *___ next week*: all gap choices with *—*) and 2 corrections (*See you on next Monday / Friday*). So *next* was used three times, *this* and *every* never, *yesterday* never.

**Mix (N = 5):** 2 choice (`mc`), 2 word bank, 1 whole sentence. No word order and no typed gap, with reasons:
- Word order: the cards contain no preposition, so putting *i check my email every morning* in order does not need the point at all (brief, point 3: a word order only where the order needs the point).
- Typed gap: the right answer is "no word", and a learner cannot type *—* (the grader has no empty answer). Typing the time word instead (*___ week*) would test vocabulary, not the point.
- The whole sentence is a third *use* task, the only kind where the learner writes the form.

Each task covers a word the point had not used yet: *last* (in a whole-sentence choice), *this*, *every*, *tomorrow* in a bank, *yesterday*. Settings in order: work, free time, technology, town / lifestyle, travel. All sentences are 5-7 words, present simple or past simple.

## Task by task: what the learner must know, and why the wrong options compete
1. *Which sentence has a mistake?* → *I started a new job on last Monday.* The learner must know that *last* + day takes no *on*. The three correct sentences are about three other things: a clock time (*at nine*), a month (*in February*), a date (*on 3 June*). So each uses a preposition correctly, and the learner cannot pick "the one with a preposition".
2. *Are you free ___ this weekend?* (— / at / on / in): *at* competes because *at the weekend* is the British form on the card; *on* is the American *on the weekend*. With *this*, none fits.
3. Bank *I check my email ___ every morning.* (— / in): *in* competes because *in the morning* is the form learners know; *every* removes it. *on / at every morning* left out: nobody writes them.
4. Bank *The new gym opens ___ tomorrow.* (— / on / in): *on tomorrow* is the typical mistake of the map (*on* + a day); *in* is the "future = in" mistake (*in two days*).
5. *Correct the mistake: We arrived in Rome on yesterday.* → *We arrived in Rome yesterday.* | *Yesterday we arrived in Rome.* The only sensible correction is to remove *on*; *in Rome* is correct and stays (it also shows that a preposition is right with a place).
